# Game theory for the portfolio problem: investor versus Nature

These notes explain, step by step, how the portfolio problem can be read as a game between an investor and "Nature", and how this connects to the distributionally robust portfolios in this project. A data-science background is enough; no game theory is assumed. Each section says where a claim is **verified numerically** (by `scripts/run_game.py` and `tests/test_game.py`) and where it is only **argued**.

## 1. Zero-sum games and saddle points

A **zero-sum game** has two players with opposite interests. Here the *investor* picks a decision (a portfolio) and *Nature* picks a state of the world (a scenario, an era, a vector of true expected returns). The **payoff** is a number the investor wants large and Nature wants small (for example the Sharpe ratio). A pair of strategies is a **saddle point** if neither player can gain by changing their own strategy alone: the investor's choice is the best response to Nature's, and Nature's is the best response to the investor's. At a saddle point the payoff is the **value** of the game.

## 2. The payoff matrix, pure and mixed strategies

With finitely many portfolios (rows `i`) and scenarios (columns `j`) we write the payoffs in a matrix `A`, `A[i,j]` = payoff of portfolio `i` in scenario `j`. A **pure strategy** is one row (or column). A **mixed strategy** is a probability vector: `x` over rows for the investor, `y` over columns for Nature, and the expected payoff is `x' A y`. Mixing matters because a game without a pure saddle point can still have a saddle point in mixed strategies. In our setting a mixed strategy for the investor can be read as splitting capital across portfolios.

## 3. The minimax theorem and why linear programming gives it

If the investor assumes Nature is hostile, the investor can guarantee `v` by choosing `x` so that every column pays at least `v`. The best guarantee solves the linear program

    investor:  maximise v   subject to   sum_i x_i A[i,j] >= v  for every column j,   sum x = 1,   x >= 0.

Nature, wanting to hold the investor's payoff down, solves

    Nature:    minimise u   subject to   sum_j A[i,j] y_j <= u  for every row i,       sum y = 1,   y >= 0.

These two programs are **dual** to each other (the dual variables of the first program's `n` constraints are Nature's probabilities `y_j`). Weak duality is easy to see: for any feasible `x, y`, `v <= x'Ay <= u`. Strong duality for linear programs says the optimal values are equal. That equality is **von Neumann's minimax theorem**: `max over x of min over j (x'A)_j = min over y of max over i (Ay)_i`. At the optimum each strategy is a best response to the other.

## 4. Wald versus Savage

Two classical rules choose a *pure* portfolio when we refuse to put probabilities on the scenarios.

* **Wald's maximin** takes the worst payoff of each portfolio over the scenarios and chooses the portfolio whose worst payoff is highest. It is pessimistic in absolute terms.
* **Savage's minimax regret** first builds the **regret matrix** `R[i,j] = max_k A[k,j] - A[i,j]` (how much better the best portfolio would have done in scenario `j`), then chooses the portfolio whose largest regret is smallest. Every column of `R` has a zero (the best portfolio has no regret) and no entry is negative.

The rules can disagree. Here is a concrete example (generated by the code, and reproduced in `tests/test_game.py`):

<!-- teaching-example:start -->
| | scenario 1 | scenario 2 | worst payoff | regret in 1 | regret in 2 | maximum regret |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 10 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 4 | 4 |
| B | 6 | 5 | 5 | 4 | 0 | 4 |
| C | 8 | 4 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 2 |

Wald maximin chooses **B** (best worst-case payoff 5). Savage minimax regret chooses **C** (smallest maximum regret 2). The two rules disagree.
<!-- teaching-example:end -->

Maximin protects against the worst absolute outcome and so picks B, which never pays less than 5 but gives up a lot in scenario 1. Minimax regret picks C, which is never far from the best choice in either scenario. Neither is "right"; they answer different questions.

In this project two payoff matrices are used (Sharpe ratios). The **six-scenario matrix** from the sensitivity task is degenerate: the max-Sharpe portfolio is the best choice in all six scenarios, so its regret row is all zeros, every rule selects it and the game has a pure saddle point. The **era matrix** evaluates five portfolios computed once on the full sample under the parameters of each era; this evaluation is **in-sample for each era** (the full-sample weights saw that era), so it illustrates the decision rules and is not a performance claim. In the run recorded in the results log the era matrix is **degenerate in the same way**: max-Sharpe has the highest Sharpe ratio in all three eras, so Wald and Savage again choose it, both linear programs return it as a pure strategy, and Nature's best reply is simply the era in which it does worst. The only matrix in this project on which Wald and Savage genuinely disagree is the constructed example above. A matrix with a real trade-off would need portfolios or scenarios in which the best portfolio changes from column to column.

## 5. The ellipsoid game (convex-concave)

Now let Nature choose the vector of true expected returns `mu` from an **ellipsoid** around the estimate `mu_hat`:

    E = { mu : (mu - mu_hat)' Sigma_mu^{-1} (mu - mu_hat) <= kappa^2 },     Sigma_mu = Sigma / T,

with `T` the number of years of data (the covariance of a sample mean). The investor picks weights `w` (non-negative, summing to one, each at most the cap). The payoff is

    f(w, mu) = mu'w - (lambda/2) w'Sigma w.

It is linear in `mu` and concave in `w`, over compact convex sets: a convex-concave game, so the order of "max over `w`" and "min over `mu`" does not matter (a minimax equality holds). This is **argued** from the structure and **verified numerically** by solving both orders.

**Inner minimum (Nature's best response to `w`).** Write `mu = mu_hat + Sigma_mu^{1/2} u` with `||u|| <= kappa`. Then `mu'w = mu_hat'w + u' Sigma_mu^{1/2} w`, and by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality this is smallest when `u` points opposite to `Sigma_mu^{1/2} w`:

    min over mu in E of mu'w = mu_hat'w - kappa sqrt(w' Sigma_mu w),
    attained at   mu*(w) = mu_hat - kappa Sigma_mu w / sqrt(w' Sigma_mu w).

So Nature lowers the expected return of the portfolio by `kappa sqrt(w'Sigma_mu w)`, moving each asset's mean most where the portfolio holds the most risk.

**Max-min (investor first).** The investor maximises `mu_hat'w - kappa sqrt(w' Sigma_mu w) - (lambda/2) w'Sigma w`. The repository code solves this with its own quadratic-programming engine through an auxiliary scale `t`: since `kappa sqrt(a) = min over t > 0 of (kappa/2)(a/t + t)`, the problem becomes a joint maximisation over `(w, t)` of `mu_hat'w - (1/2)(lambda + kappa/(tT)) w'Sigma w - kappa t / 2`, which is jointly concave. For a fixed `t` the problem in `w` is an ordinary mean-variance quadratic program with a larger risk aversion `lambda + kappa/(tT)`; a one-dimensional search over `t` finishes the job, and at the optimum `t = sqrt(w*'Sigma_mu w*)`.

**Min-max (Nature first).** For a fixed `mu` the best portfolio solves a quadratic program, and by quadratic-programming duality its value equals the minimum, over multipliers `nu` (free) and `alpha, beta >= 0`, of `nu + cap * sum(beta) + (1/(2 lambda)) q' Sigma^{-1} q` with `q = mu - nu 1 + alpha - beta`. Minimising over `mu` in the ellipsoid as well gives one convex program. This is solved by an independent convex-programming routine; its role is **verification only**.

**The saddle point `(w*, mu*)`** has `mu* = mu*(w*)` and `w*` a best response to `mu*`. Numerically checked for each `kappa`: both orders give the same value; `mu*` lies on the boundary of the ellipsoid; `w*` is a best response to `mu*` (a quadratic program solved separately); `mu*` is a best response to `w*` (closed form, and no random or locally perturbed boundary point does better); the weights equal those of the second-order-cone program in `src/robust.py`; and at `kappa = 0` the solution is the ordinary plug-in mean-variance portfolio.

**Price of robustness** is the plug-in value (the payoff at `mu_hat` with the plug-in portfolio) minus the saddle value; it is zero at `kappa = 0` and grows with `kappa`. The tables also report how far Nature moves `mu` (in standard errors) and how the portfolio changes (positions, effective number of positions, L1 distance from the plug-in).

## 6. The Wasserstein ball as Nature's strategy set

The DR-CVaR portfolios in `src/wdro.py` let Nature choose any distribution `Q` within transport distance `eps` of the empirical distribution of the `T` daily return vectors, where moving probability mass from `xi` to `xi'` costs `||xi - xi'||` in the ground norm chosen in the configuration (norm 1, whose dual norm is the maximum). The investor's loss is `L = -w'xi` and the criterion is its CVaR at tail probability `alpha = 1 - 0.95`.

**Upper bound (the dual LP).** For a fixed portfolio, the worst case over the ball is bounded above by a finite linear program: minimise `lam*eps + (1/T) sum_i s_i` subject to `s_i >= tau + (1/alpha)(L_i - tau)` and `s_i >= tau` (written through the coefficients of `src/wdro.py`) and `lam >= (1/alpha) ||w||_dual`. It is an upper bound on the worst-case CVaR because the threshold `tau` is chosen before Nature moves.

**Lower bound (an explicit Nature strategy).** We construct a distribution inside the ball and compute its CVaR. Let `u` be the unit vector (in the ground norm) that maximises `w'u`; for norm 1 this is a unit step along the asset with the largest weight, and `w'u = ||w||_dual`. Moving a return vector by `-delta u` raises its loss by exactly `delta ||w||_dual`. The CVaR at level `alpha` is the average of the worst `alpha` of the probability mass. So Nature takes exactly that worst `alpha` of the mass (the worst `floor(alpha T)` points and, when `alpha T` is not an integer, the needed fraction of the next point, whose mass is split) and moves it by `delta = eps/alpha`. The cost is the moved mass times the distance, `alpha * (eps/alpha) = eps`: the whole budget and no more. The moved mass was already the worst `alpha` and rises by the same amount, so it stays the worst `alpha`, and the CVaR of the new distribution is

    CVaR_Q = empirical CVaR + (eps / alpha) ||w||_dual.

**What the numbers show.** The upper bound from the dual LP, the closed form above and the CVaR of the constructed distribution coincide to rounding error for every portfolio and radius tested (see the generated block in section 9), the constructed distribution uses the entire budget, and random feasible strategies fall far short. Therefore, in this formulation the worst-case CVaR equals "empirical CVaR plus `eps ||w||_dual / alpha`" **exactly**, not only as a bound. This is **argued** by the construction above (a loss that is piecewise linear in `xi`, with all the movable mass pushed along one best direction) and **verified numerically**.

**What this does and does not verify.** Equality of the two bounds shows Nature's best response to a given portfolio and the value of the inner maximisation, so the interchange of "choose `tau`" and "Nature chooses `Q`" loses nothing at these portfolios. It does not by itself show that the DR-CVaR portfolio is a best response to Nature's constructed distribution, which a full saddle-point statement would also need; that second half is **not** checked here.

## 7. How this connects to the DRO portfolio

The distributionally robust portfolio is the investor's side of a game: the investor picks `w`, Nature picks a distribution (or, in section 5, a mean vector) in an uncertainty set, and the investor minimises the worst case. The ellipsoid game is the same idea with an uncertainty set on the mean only; its price of robustness is the cost of insuring against estimation error. The Wasserstein game puts the uncertainty on the whole distribution; the exact formula above shows that its worst case is the empirical CVaR plus a penalty proportional to `eps` times the dual norm of `w`, which is why the DR-CVaR portfolio spreads weight across assets as `eps` grows (the dual norm for norm 1 is the largest weight).
